Stacks issued **14.97M STX** in the last 90 days through two separate protocol mints, and absorbed **none** of it: STX has no buyback, no burn, and no lock that takes coins off the market. The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads STX at **+0.80% net** over the trailing window and **+1.23%** forward, against a supply-monitor reading of **+0.836%**. The forward figure is higher because a **Jul 30 2026** upgrade raised both mints on the same day and deleted the reduction schedule that was supposed to keep cutting one of them. STX is **uncapped**, and since that upgrade there is no scheduled step-down left in the code.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

Over the last 90 days the MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads Stacks at **+0.80% net**: **14.97M STX** of newly minted supply against **zero** on the buy side, on a circulating base of **1,862.2M STX**. The supply monitor reads the same trailing window at **+0.836%**, a gap of **0.03 percentage points** — well inside tolerance, so this build ships **no monitor-gap chip** and needs no reconciliation walk. That agreement is expected rather than impressive: for a coin whose only ledger movement is issuance, both sides are measuring the same quantity from two directions. Forward, the framework reads **+1.23%**, re-based on the post-upgrade rate rather than the blended trailing average. Stacks is a **one-sided ledger** — a Bitcoin-anchored chain whose entire Pressure Framework reading is decided by how fast it mints, because nothing on the coin ever removes a single STX.

## Sell pressure: where new STX comes from

Stacks runs **two** mints at once, and they do not share a clock — the single most misread fact about STX supply. Sell #1, protocol inflation, is the mining coinbase: Stacks pays a fixed reward on **every Bitcoin block**, won by whichever miner takes that block's tenure. That reward had been cut to **500 STX** in April 2026 under an earlier halving-alignment proposal. The PoX-5 upgrade, activated at Bitcoin block **960,230** on **Jul 30 2026**, restored it to **1,000 STX** per Bitcoin block and removed the reduction schedule entirely. This window therefore straddles the change — roughly two months at the old rate and one at the new — for **8.48M STX** of mining coinbase.

Sell #5, the endowment emission, is the second mint and the one most coverage of Stacks leaves out. A community vote created a five-year ecosystem endowment funded by fresh issuance, paid not per Bitcoin block but at **every mining tenure** — and the same Jul 30 2026 upgrade day stepped that payment from **475 STX** to **1,140 STX**. Reading the chain confirms it exactly: every coinbase transaction credits the endowment contract precisely **1,140** STX. That is **6.49M STX** over the window. The denominators genuinely differ — only about **three** Bitcoin blocks in **four** won a tenure across this window, and closer to **two** in **three** since the upgrade — so scaling one mint from the other overstates the endowment by roughly a third. The two together are the entire sell side, and they close on the chain's own supply reading to within **1%**.

The other three sell rows are all **zero**, and each for a checkable reason. Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is zero because the chain reports **100%** of STX unlocked at both ends of the window — the escrow from the 2019 offering is fully spent, and the reduction ladder that unlock trackers still publish was deleted from the protocol on Jul 30 2026 and was never an unlock to begin with. Sell #3, foundation and unscheduled unlocks, is covered in its own section below. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy, is zero because Stacks has no bankruptcy estate, no trustee schedule and no court-ordered distribution of any kind.

## Buy pressure: where new STX goes

Every buy row on Stacks is **zero**, and that is the finding, not a gap in the research. Buy #1, programmatic buyback, is zero because no contract, treasury programme or announced schedule buys STX on the open market — the endowment is funded by printing STX, not by purchasing it. Buy #2, protocol fee burn, is zero, and this build checked both surfaces a burn can hide behind rather than trusting total supply alone: the unspendable address gained **0.98 STX** across the whole 90 days, and total STX supply rose at every sampled point and never once fell. What Proof of Transfer consumes is **bitcoin**, never STX — and the PoX-5 upgrade stopped even that, routing previously-burned miner bitcoin into the reward pool instead. Buy #3, foundation buy, is zero with no discretionary accumulation observed in the window.

Buy #4 is where Stacks invites a mistake, so it is worth being precise. Stacking commits STX to the protocol and pays a real yield — but the yield is paid in **bitcoin**, not in STX, so no stacking return belongs anywhere on this page as buy pressure. Nor is the commitment a lock in the framework's sense: it runs for a roughly two-week reward cycle and then rolls off, and committed STX stays inside the counted circulating figure the whole time. Measured at both ends of the window regardless, the committed total fell from **504.26M STX** to **421.49M STX**, a decline of **82.77M**. Read generously it removes nothing; read literally it went the other way.

## Foundation and overhang

Four endowment-controlled balances are watched on Stacks, all read on-chain at both ends of this window. The endowment's escrow contract fell from **110.93M STX** to **58.99M**; its main treasury rose from **18.49M** to **45.94M**; an operating wallet fell from **7.84M** to **2.69M**; and a retired wallet sits effectively empty and did not move. The cluster held **107.62M STX** at the end of the window against **137.26M** at the start — down **29.63M**, and down roughly **36.12M** once the endowment mint flowing in is netted out. That is a real, sizeable, discretionary deployment, and it is **5.8%** of circulating STX still sitting in those wallets.

Sell #3 nonetheless books **zero**, and the reason is arithmetic rather than generosity. Those coins were minted long ago and the circulating figure this page divides by already counts every one of them, so deploying them changes the float's composition and not its size. Booking the outflow anyway would put the trailing reading at **+2.74%** against a monitor reading of **+0.836%** — a gap invented by counting the same STX twice. What the row does carry is the watch: if the escrow, the treasury or the operating wallet keeps falling between refreshes, the outflow enters Sell #3 at the next refresh.

## How STX compares to other Bitcoin-anchored chains

The obvious comparison is to Bitcoin itself, and it is the comparison Stacks has spent years inviting. Bitcoin's issuance is a hard cap plus a halving written into consensus code that no vote reaches; Stacks anchors to Bitcoin, times its blocks to Bitcoin, pays its stackers in bitcoin — and then issues STX on a schedule that a governance vote can and did rewrite. In April 2026 a proposal cut the coinbase in half in explicit alignment with Bitcoin's halving rhythm. In July 2026 a different proposal put it back and deleted the ladder. Both were legitimate community decisions passed with overwhelming support. Both also demonstrate that on STX the emission schedule is a **parameter**, not a constant, which is the opposite of the asset it settles against.

Against uncapped continuous-emission layer ones, STX looks comparatively restrained on the headline number. A **+1.23%** forward reading per 90 days is roughly **5%** annualised — unremarkable next to chains that mint several times that. The structural difference is what sits opposite the mint. Chains with a fee burn convert usage directly into supply reduction, and the busier they get the more they take back; exchange tokens with quarterly buybacks convert revenue into permanent destruction. Stacks converts usage into **bitcoin** for stackers and miners. That is a genuine product, and it is worth nothing at all to STX supply, which is why the buy column on this page is four zeroes rather than a small number.

The comparison that matters most is with STX's own recent past. Stacks is still widely described as a halving-model chain with a coinbase stepping down toward 2028 and 2032. That model was removed on **Jul 30 2026**. A reader carrying it forward will under-forecast STX issuance by roughly half, and will miss the endowment mint — a second, tenure-indexed stream that is **43%** of all new STX — entirely.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, the PoX-6 proposal. The **1,000 STX** per Bitcoin block rate is explicitly described as provisional for the current bootstrap phase, with a formal reassessment of the emission schedule promised in the next proposal — the single change that could move Sell #1 materially in either direction. Second, the endowment cluster's balances: **107.62M STX** across four wallets, spending down at roughly **36M** a quarter, with a monthly escrow release of about **4.17M STX** continuing into mid-2027. Third, the Genesis Bond, announced **Aug 17 2026** with enrollment opening **Sep 10 2026**: it targets **100** to **200** BTC at roughly **3%** yield and is denominated in bitcoin end to end, so it should mint, burn and lock no STX at all — worth confirming it does not. Fourth, a 90-day DeFi incentive programme from around **Sep 19 2026** paying **1** BTC a month, again in bitcoin. Fifth, whether the stacked total keeps falling after **82.77M STX** came uncommitted this window.

## Summary

Stacks has a one-sided supply ledger. Two protocol mints — a **1,000 STX** coinbase on every Bitcoin block and a **1,140 STX** endowment payment at every mining tenure — printed **14.97M STX** in 90 days, against **zero** buyback, **zero** burn and **zero** net locking, for **+0.80% net** and a projected **+1.23%** forward. The structural mechanism worth remembering is that the two mints run on different denominators, so they cannot be scaled from one another, and that the **Jul 30 2026** upgrade raised both on the same day while deleting the only scheduled reduction Stacks had. The key risk is that there is no offset to fail — the buy side cannot weaken because it does not exist, and the entire reading turns on a governance parameter that has already been changed twice in one year. There is no ceiling either: STX has no maximum supply, and the figure often quoted as one is a projection rather than a cap.
