# QNT Inflation Analysis · August 2026 · Mixed flows, supply roughly steady

Quant issued QNT once, in a single Ethereum token sale in **2018**, and over the 90 days to **Aug 26 2026** the Pressure Framework measures **0 QNT** of new supply against **0 QNT** removed — a net of **0.00%** on a circulating base of **14.54M QNT**, against our supply monitor's **+0.01%**, a gap of **0.01 percentage points** that raises no flag. Those zeros are measured rather than assumed: every Transfer event the QNT contract has ever emitted was replayed, and the only **608** that created tokens all fall inside two days of July 2018. The caveat that matters is structural, not numerical — the QNT contract's **mint** function is still live behind a closed 2018 sale contract, so Quant is **a fixed-supply token by practice rather than by protocol**.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

For the 90-day window ending **Aug 26 2026**, the MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads **QNT at 0.00% net**: sell pressure of **0 QNT** against buy pressure of **0 QNT**, on a circulating base of **14.54M QNT**. Our supply monitor reads **+0.01%** for the same window, a gap of **0.01 percentage points**, far inside the half-point tolerance, so no monitor-gap flag ships. That residual is arithmetic, not supply: the monitor infers supply by dividing market capitalisation by price, and with QNT quoted at **$63.32** — four significant figures — the derived figure wanders across a band of about **67,920 QNT** from day to day and flips sign between **−0.04%** and **+0.08%** at random. Its 95-day median lands within **466 QNT** of the published circulating figure. QNT is best labelled **a quiet, one-shot token whose ledger is empty on both sides and whose only real risk is an unrenounced mint switch**.

## Sell pressure: where new QNT comes from

Nowhere, in this window. Sell #1, protocol inflation, is **0 QNT**, and the way that zero was established is the whole point of this page. Reading a supply number at two dates and finding it unchanged proves very little, and in Quant's case it proves nothing at all: the QNT contract's **totalSupply** is a constant written into the deployed source in 2018 and never updated by any function, which is why block explorers still display **45,467,000 QNT** for a token whose real issuance was **24,431,259**. It is an identity, not a ledger. So instead of trusting it, every **Transfer** event QNT has ever emitted was swept from block **5,000,000** to today across 2,084 contiguous ranges. The result: **608** mint events in the token's entire life, every one of them between blocks **5,851,179** and **5,852,490** in July 2018, and none since. A second sweep at a different range width returned an identical list, and an unfiltered replay of all **127,265** QNT transfers inside the window independently confirms that no QNT was minted between **May 28 2026** and **Aug 26 2026**.

That said, the QNT contract can still mint. The verified source carries **mint(address, uint256)** restricted to a single crowdsale contract, and that address was written once in the constructor with no setter — mint authority was parked, not renounced. The crowdsale contract is still deployed and readable, and at both ends of the window it reports its sale switches closed but its allowances intact: a regular-sale ceiling of **22,466,887 QNT** against **1,431,147** actually sold, leaving **21,035,741 QNT** of unspent mint headroom behind an owner-only function call. Because mint credits balances without touching the hard-coded total, a future mint would not even move the supply figure. Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is **0** because Quant's only lock was a 12-month schedule for staff after the 2018 sale, complete since 2019, with no vesting contract on chain. Sell #3, Foundation and unscheduled unlocks, is **0** — no public evidence of release in window. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy, is **0**: there is no estate and no trustee attached to QNT.

## Buy pressure: where new QNT goes

Also nowhere. Buy #1, programmatic buyback, is **0 QNT**: Quant invoices Overledger licences in pounds and keeps the proceeds as company revenue, and the project's own transparency filing states that holders have no claim on revenue and that no mechanism routes any of it back to QNT. Buy #2, protocol fee burn, is **0** for a stronger reason than absence of policy — the deployed QNT contract has no burn entry point at all, and it is not a proxy, so one cannot be added. This is the single row on the page that genuinely is permanent. What Quant calls its 2018 burn was in fact a transfer: at block **6,331,129** the company address sent **9,545,765.95 QNT** to the QNT token's own address, which has no owner, no rescue and no withdraw function, so the balance is unrecoverable by destination rather than destroyed by opcode. Users have since posted a further **5,116 QNT** into it by mistake, **11.8** of that inside this window, and its outflow over the same 90 days is exactly **zero**.

Buy #3, Foundation buy, is **0** — no Quant wallet is published and no dated purchase could be confirmed. Buy #4, new long-term lock, is the row that the Quant story leads you to expect, and it is **0** too. The Overledger licence model is supposed to lock QNT in on-chain treasury contracts for the licence term, and those contracts genuinely exist: Quant's own repository publishes four mainnet treasury proxies, each confirmed to hold real contract code before any balance was read. Every one of them is an upgradeable proxy, so their implementations were resolved and their verified interfaces pulled. Read at both ends of the window, the factory counts **zero** registered gateways and **zero** registered applications, the deposit contract reports a QNT balance of **zero**, and all four addresses hold **nothing**. The reads are live — the same contracts return the correct QNT token address, treasury address and operator address on the same call path — so the licence sink is not broken, it is simply unused. Node staking under the Trusted Node Program remains roadmap: no published stake size, no deployed contract, no dated launch, and therefore no figure booked in advance of it.

## Foundation and overhang

Quant's overhang is real, small, and — this is the honest finding — **unlocated**. The published total supply of **14,612,493 QNT** minus the published circulating supply of **14,544,176 QNT** leaves **68,317 QNT** classified as held back, which matches Quant's own filing describing roughly **68,000 QNT** of unlocked company-held tokens that can be sold at any time. But Quant publishes no wallet address, and the one address the chain can attribute to the company — the address that received the **14,467,000 QNT** allocation in 2018 and later sent the **9.55M** write-off — holds **1 QNT** at both ends of the window and has not sent anything since 2019. Its full outgoing history closes exactly against its allocation across **36** transfers, so the reserve is somewhere else entirely. That subtraction failing to close is the finding: the overhang is carried here as an opaque bucket, tracked through disclosure rather than through a balance.

The rest of the enumeration is a set of measured absences. There is no Quant DAO and no DAO treasury — the filing is explicit that holders have no rights over any treasury. The four Overledger treasury contracts are the only identified protocol-controlled addresses and they hold nothing. The window's transfers were aggregated by sender rather than by balance, because a wallet can receive more than it pays out while still selling: the largest true net decreases were **307,233 QNT** from an exchange hot wallet, **117,860** from an unidentified address and **35,000** from a private holder, and none of them is attributable to Quant. The round-trip control caught what a gross reading would have invented — one exchange wallet moved **499,784 QNT** out and **491,529** back in for a net of **−8,255**. If the undisclosed company reserve is ever spent, or the crowdsale owner ever re-opens the sale, the outflow enters Sell #3 at the next refresh.

## How QNT compares to other fixed-supply ERC-20 tokens

QNT belongs to a small class: tokens whose entire supply was created in one issuance event and whose contract has neither an emission schedule nor a burn. That is a different shape from an exchange token, where a quarterly buyback and burn funded by trading revenue makes the supply curve slope downward year after year — Quant deliberately does not do this, because Overledger revenue is billed in fiat and legally belongs to the company, not to the token. It is also different from a fee-burning smart-contract platform, where every transaction destroys part of the fee and the supply oscillates with network usage; QNT is a plain ERC-20 with no fee mechanism of its own. And it is different again from a halving-model proof-of-work chain, which has a hard cap but keeps issuing against it for decades. QNT is already at its terminal supply, and has been since 2018.

The comparison that actually matters is with tokens whose scarcity is enforced by code versus tokens whose scarcity is enforced by conduct. A token whose mint authority has been renounced on chain cannot be inflated by anyone, ever. QNT is not in that category: its mint function survives, its authority holder is a live contract, and that contract's owner can re-open a sale that still has **21,035,741 QNT** of unused ceiling. Eight years of not doing it is meaningful evidence of intent, and nothing here suggests it is about to change — but it is evidence about behaviour, not a constraint in the code. The other structural quirk is classification, and it is larger than it looks. Measured issuance of **24,431,259 QNT** less the **9,545,766** written off in 2018 leaves **14,885,493** on chain, but the published total supply is **14,612,493** — exactly **273,000 QNT** lower, tokens that were minted, never destroyed and simply are not in the project's own figure. Because the contract's supply constant was never updated, different data providers land in different places: one major aggregator currently lists total supply **268,871 QNT** higher and circulating supply **2.47M** lower than the figures used here.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, the crowdsale contract's sale switches. Both read closed today; either flipping to open would be the first genuine issuance signal in eight years, and it is a single owner transaction away. Second, the Trusted Node Program, which follows the Fusion Rollup mainnet launch of **Jun 2 2026** and is expected to introduce QNT staking — the number to watch is a published minimum stake and a deployed staking contract, either of which would open Buy #4 for the first time. Third, the Overledger treasury contracts: any non-zero gateway count or deposit balance would mean the licence-locking sink has finally started working. Fourth, the undisclosed company reserve of roughly **68,317 QNT** — Quant has no obligation to publish a wallet, so this will be tracked through disclosure and through any change in the classified circulating figure. Fifth, whether either aggregator revises its QNT supply classification; a reclassification would move the denominator without moving a single token.

## Summary

The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads QNT at **0.00% net** over the 90 days to **Aug 26 2026**, with both sides of the ledger empty and every zero established by replaying the QNT contract's complete transfer history rather than by comparing two supply readings. The structural mechanism is that Quant issued QNT once in 2018 and monetises Overledger through fiat licence fees, so nothing in the business either mints or retires the token. The key risk is not dilution today but the fact that dilution remains possible: the mint function is live, its authority was never renounced, and roughly **21.0M QNT** of sale ceiling sits unused behind it. The ceiling itself is conduct rather than code — QNT is fixed in supply because Quant has chosen not to issue more, which has held for eight years and is the single assumption this reading rests on.
