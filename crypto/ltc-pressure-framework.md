> Originally published at **[mrnasdog.com/research/ltc/inflation](https://mrnasdog.com/research/ltc/inflation)** by MrNasdog.

Litecoin (LTC) mints **0.322M LTC** every 90 days from a fixed proof-of-work block subsidy of 6.25 LTC, and the Litecoin protocol offsets none of it — no buyback, no fee burn, no staking lock, no burn address anywhere in the design. The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads **+0.41% net** against a **77.55M** circulating base and an 84M hard cap, with the independent supply monitor at **+0.44%** — a gap of **0.03 percentage points**. About 92% of all Litecoin is already mined, and no halving falls inside the window behind or the window ahead: the next Litecoin halving is roughly a year out, around **Jul 2027**.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

For the 90-day window ending **Aug 29 2026**, the framework reads **LTC at +0.41% net inflation**: clockwork mining emission against a buy ledger that is completely empty. The independent supply monitor reads **+0.44%** over the same window — a gap of **0.03 percentage points**, far inside the half-point tolerance, so no monitor-gap warning is raised on the Litecoin reading. Both readings count the same thing, newly minted Litecoin, and the residual is ordinary snapshot noise: the monitor infers supply from market data, so its start and end points sit slightly off the exact block heights the framework measures. Litecoin is a **quiet chain with a predictable, decaying issuance curve** — fifteen years of proof-of-work mining edging toward a fixed ceiling, with nothing discretionary anywhere in the supply picture.

## Sell pressure: where new LTC comes from

Litecoin has exactly one mint, and Sell #1, protocol inflation, is it: **0.322M LTC** over the window. The number is measured from the chain rather than assumed from the parameter, and on Litecoin that distinction changes the answer. Litecoin targets a 2.5-minute block, which would imply 576 blocks in each 24 hours, but the chain actually sealed **51,454** blocks between the two window boundaries — one every **151 seconds**, about **572** a day, or 99.25% of nominal. Litecoin ran slightly slow, so the parameter read would have overstated the emission by 0.75%. Multiplied by the **6.25 LTC** coinbase subsidy that is **321,588 LTC** of brand-new Litecoin paid to Scrypt miners. Cumulative-subsidy arithmetic at the two block heights returns the identical figure, and Litecoin's on-chain supply sits 0.006% **below** the running total of every block subsidy ever paid — proof that no LTC has ever been minted outside the block reward, including through the MWEB validation bug patched earlier in 2026.

The halving is worth reading off the chain too, because Litecoin's subsidy cut fires **at** the round block, not after it. At the last Litecoin halving boundary, block 2,519,999 paid a **12.5 LTC** subsidy and block 2,520,000 paid **6.25 LTC** — the step happened on the boundary block itself. The next boundary is block **3,360,000**, where the subsidy halves to 3.125 LTC. At the measured block rate that is roughly **191,600** blocks and about a year away, so it lands in neither the trailing 90-day window nor the forward one, and the Litecoin emission rate carried forward is the same 6.25 LTC that produced the trailing number.

Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is **0** and always will be. Litecoin launched in Oct 2011 as a fair launch: no presale, no insider allocation, no investor tranche, therefore no vesting contract and no unlock calendar that could ever fire. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy, is also **0** — no estate, no trustee schedule and no escrowed block of LTC exists anywhere in the coin's history to unwind into the market. Sell #3, Foundation and unscheduled unlocks, is **0** as well: the Litecoin Foundation is a donation-funded non-profit that holds no protocol allocation, so the surprise-distribution risk that shadows most tokens simply does not exist here.

## Buy pressure: where new LTC goes

Nowhere, and this build checked that from both directions rather than one. Buy #2, protocol fee burn, is **0** on two separate surfaces. On the address side, Litecoin has no burn address and no fee sink, and reading the coinbase at both window ends and at four points in between shows every block paying the **6.25 LTC** subsidy plus the fees on top to the miner — fees are added, never destroyed. On the supply side, every published Litecoin supply meter rose across the window and none fell. There is a third case specific to a chain like Litecoin: coins sent to keyless, provably unspendable addresses are gone forever, but they are still counted in the published Litecoin supply, because that figure is an issuance meter rather than a sum of spendable coins — which is exactly why it reads below the subsidy ceiling instead of netting anything out. So even those cannot land in this row. Buy #2 is a documented zero, not an unexamined one.

The other three are zero by design. Buy #1, programmatic buyback, is **0**: Litecoin captures no protocol revenue and holds no treasury contract, so there is nothing to fund a buyback with and no contract on the chain that could execute one. Buy #3, Foundation buy, is **0**: the Litecoin Foundation runs on donations, grants and merchandise and has never announced or executed an open-market accumulation programme. Buy #4, new long-term lock, is **0** because proof-of-work Litecoin has no staking, no lockup contract and no vault. Litecoin's optional privacy extension is not a lock either — moving LTC into it is a transfer between address types, so those coins stay in circulating supply and belong in neither ledger.

## Foundation and overhang

Three identified overhangs are tracked on Litecoin, and none of them adds supply. The Litecoin Foundation holds no protocol allocation and publishes no current LTC treasury figure; it is donation-funded, has never shown a pattern of market selling, and is walked by hand rather than read on-chain. The second is a frozen recovery output: an MWEB validation bug at block 3,073,882 in Mar 2026 let an attacker inflate a pegout to **85,034 LTC** from an input worth barely 1.2 LTC, the coins were returned and the full amount was pegged back into the extension at block **3,078,098**, where the resulting output was frozen by consensus. It is identified and coordinated, so the framework enumerates it — but it cannot move, and both events sit before this window opens. The third is the material one: a Nasdaq-listed Litecoin treasury company still held **819,070 LTC** — a little over 1% of circulating Litecoin — at its last disclosure on Jul 17 2026, and has been selling to fund a share-repurchase programme, refreshed through quarterly and event filings rather than a live wallet feed. That balance is enumerated and watched, because a block of coins over 1% of supply in one hand is a real price risk, but it is watched rather than added: the test is direction, not actor. A move out of a project reserve into circulation is booked; a listed company trading coins it bought on the open market with shareholder capital is not, for the same reason an exchange's customer wallet is not. If any of these three balances falls between refreshes, the outflow enters Sell #3 at the next refresh.

## How LTC compares to other hard-capped proof-of-work chains

Litecoin belongs to the smallest and cleanest supply class in the framework: fair-launch, hard-capped, halving proof-of-work. Its structural analogues are Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash, and the mechanism is identical in shape — a coinbase subsidy that is the only mint, cut in half at a fixed block height, with fees paid to miners rather than burned. Litecoin sits further along that curve than most: **92%** of the 84M Litecoin cap is already mined, so each remaining halving removes a smaller absolute quantity of new supply than the last. What separates this class from uncapped continuous-emission chains is the absence of discretion. A delegated proof-of-stake L1 can change its emission by governance vote; Litecoin cannot, short of a hard fork that miners would have to adopt. Even the Litecoin Core release that activated a new consensus rule inside this window was pure validation hardening for the privacy extension — the subsidy, the halving schedule and the fee policy were untouched.

The contrast with exchange tokens and fee-burning smart-contract platforms is sharper still. Those assets can and do run the buy side — quarterly revenue buybacks, base-fee burns, staking locks — which is how they reach a negative net reading. Litecoin has no mechanism capable of producing one. Every asset with a hard cap eventually stops inflating, but only assets with a live sink can actively shrink, and Litecoin has never had a sink. Its fee economy is also among the smallest in coverage relative to market value, which means a fee burn would barely register even if the Litecoin protocol adopted one. The result is an asset whose inflation is low, honest and completely predictable, but which can never print a deflationary quarter on mechanism alone.

Against privacy coins with permanent tail emission, Litecoin looks strictly better on supply: the tail-emission model floors inflation at a small positive number forever, while Litecoin's number keeps halving toward zero. Against Bitcoin, the difference is timing rather than kind — the same clockwork, one halving cycle out of phase.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, the Litecoin treasury company's next filing: its fiscal year ended Jun 30 2026, so an annual report due around late Sep 2026 is the next hard datapoint on whether the sale rate held, accelerated or stopped — it will not change the Litecoin supply number, but it sizes the overhang. Second, whether that share-repurchase authorisation is extended or exhausted, which decides how much of the remaining **819,070 LTC** is likely to reach the order book. Third, further Litecoin Core maintenance releases — three shipped in 2026, all MWEB security hardening with zero issuance impact, and the on-chain supply check confirms none created LTC; the watch line is whether any future release touches issuance rather than validation. Fourth, the frozen recovery output of **85,034 LTC**, which should stay frozen and would be a material event if it ever moved. Fifth, the realised Litecoin block interval itself: it ran at 151 seconds against a 150-second target this window, and a sustained shift in hashrate moves the emission a full percent either way before any halving does.

## Summary

The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads Litecoin at **+0.41% net supply growth** over 90 days and projects the same forward: **0.322M LTC** of sell pressure against a buy ledger of exactly **0**. The structure behind that number is the simplest in coverage — a 6.25 LTC proof-of-work subsidy that is the only mint Litecoin has, with no vesting, no Foundation allocation, no burn, no buyback and no staking lock anywhere in the design. The risk that does not show up in that number is the corporate one: a single Nasdaq-listed holder still controls over 1% of circulating LTC and is selling it steadily to fund its own share buyback, disclosed quarterly rather than observed live. That is an overhang on the price, not an addition to the supply, which is exactly why it is enumerated here and left out of the ledger. The ceiling is fixed at **84M LTC**, about 92% of it already mined, with the subsidy halving to 3.125 LTC at block 3,360,000, roughly a year out.
