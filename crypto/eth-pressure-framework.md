Ethereum minted about **255.7K ETH** to validators over the last 90 days and destroyed only about **3.7K ETH** through the EIP-1559 base-fee burn — the burn now covers barely one percent of what the protocol pays out. Add roughly **3.2K ETH** drained from Ethereum Foundation wallets and the net is **+0.21%** of supply to market over 90 days, with **+0.22%** projected next. ETH has no hard cap and no buyback; the only brake on issuance is the burn, and the burn has been shrinking since rollup data moved to blobspace.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

Against a circulating supply of **120.68M ETH**, the Pressure Framework reads sell pressure of **258.9K ETH** and buy pressure of **3.7K ETH** over the trailing 90 days — a net of **+0.211%**. Projected forward on the live protocol run rate, the next 90 days come to **+0.216%**. The inflation monitor reads **−0.013%** for the same window, a gap of **0.23 percentage points** — inside the framework's 0.5pp tolerance, so no data-conflict flag is raised. That monitor figure derives supply from market cap divided by price and carries roughly ±0.05% of rounding noise on an asset this size, which is why it prints near zero while Ethereum's own supply plainly rose by about **252,000 ETH** across the window. The label for Ethereum today: **mildly inflationary on a shrinking burn** — a proof-of-stake Layer 1 whose issuance is small and predictable, but whose only offset has quietly stopped working.

## Sell pressure: where new ETH comes from

Ethereum has exactly one mint. Consensus-layer issuance pays validators for attesting, proposing and sync-committee duty, and it is set by a curve rather than a schedule: issuance per epoch equals 64 multiplied by the square root of the total active balance, which in annual terms is roughly **166.32 × √(staked ETH)**. A fresh consensus-layer read this session puts the staked pool at about **42.3M ETH**, close to **35%** of the float, which the curve turns into about **1.08M ETH** a year — roughly **266.5K ETH** per 90 days at the theoretical ceiling. What the chain actually delivered was **255.7K ETH**, about **2,841 ETH a day**: the staked pool grew through the window so its average was lower than today's reading, exited validators still sit inside the balance sum, and attestation is never a perfect 100%. That measured number is the one the framework ships. Because the curve is a square root, the mint grows slowly even as staking climbs — and staking is climbing: the exit queue is effectively empty while roughly **2.48M ETH** waits to enter.

Vesting unlocks contribute nothing, and cannot. Ethereum's 2014 crowdsale and both founding endowments finished distributing in 2017; there is no vesting contract, no cliff, no team lock and no unlock calendar entry for ETH anywhere. The Foundation row does carry a figure. The Ethereum Foundation wallet cluster — a main multisig, a secondary wallet and two disbursing safes — fell from **12,692 ETH** to **9,503 ETH** across the window, a net drain of about **3.2K ETH**. Four separate 1,000-ETH tranches left the main multisig on **May 25 2026**, **Jul 15 2026**, **Jul 27 2026** and **Aug 11 2026**; each was traced on-chain, and because part of that flow landed in other Foundation-controlled safes, only the net drain is booked. The bankruptcy row is zero: the large crypto estates settle creditor claims in cash, so none of them sends ether to market on a schedule.

## Buy pressure: where new ETH goes

There is no programmatic buyback on Ethereum, and there cannot be one — validator pay is minted, not purchased, and the protocol holds no treasury it could bid with. There is no Foundation buy either; the Ethereum Foundation sells ETH to fund research, grants and donations, and has announced no accumulation programme. That leaves one buy row, the **EIP-1559 base-fee burn**, which destroys every base fee paid on Ethereum plus the blob base fees introduced by EIP-4844. Measured across the exact 90-day window it came to about **3.7K ETH** — roughly **41 ETH a day** — against issuance of 255.7K. Two independent measurements agreed: a full burn series taken day by day and converted at each day's price, checked against the chain's own 30-day burn sum to within 0.8%, and a direct per-block read of base fee multiplied by gas used across 271 sampled blocks.

Why the burn is this small is the whole story of ETH inflation in 2026. The L1 base fee sits near **0.10 gwei** against a gas limit raised to **60M**, and rollup data now lives on cheap blobspace rather than in calldata. Month by month this session, the mean base fee ran **0.19 gwei** in May, **0.33 gwei** in June, **0.12 gwei** in July and **0.10 gwei** in August. The fourth buy row, new long-term lock, is also zero. Staked ETH keeps growing, but staking here is not a lockup contract with a stated size and not a staking cap: anyone can leave through the exit queue or sell a liquid staking token instead. The growing stake is counted where it actually acts — on the mint side, raising issuance through the square-root curve.

## Foundation and overhang

Two team-controlled overhangs are tracked on Ethereum, and both belong to the Ethereum Foundation. The first is the liquid wallet cluster above, holding about **9,503 ETH** at the close of the window and read directly from chain state. It runs on no published calendar: the Foundation's treasury policy sets annual spending as a share of treasury value with a multi-year cash buffer, which triggers sales when the fiat side falls short rather than on fixed dates. The observed rhythm is roughly one 1,000-ETH tranche every three weeks. The second is the Foundation's staked position of about **70,000 ETH**, a programme completed in April 2026 and designed to fund operations from yield instead of sales; wider reporting puts total Foundation holdings near **102,400 ETH**. Ethereum has no other qualifying overhang — no unscheduled-unlock pool, no DAO treasury, no identified founder or partner wallet readable as a unit, no bankruptcy-estate residual in ETH. If either Foundation balance falls between refreshes, that outflow enters the Foundation sell row at the next refresh.

## How ETH compares to other proof-of-stake Layer 1 chains

Against a capped, halving-model chain, Ethereum is the opposite design. Bitcoin's issuance is fixed in code, halves on a known block height and terminates at 21M coins; Ethereum has no cap at all, and its issuance is a live function of how much ETH is staked. Less predictable in principle, far gentler in practice: **+0.21%** over 90 days annualises to well under 1%, below what most capped chains ran in their early schedules. The trade is that Ethereum can never promise a terminal supply.

Against uncapped continuous-emission Layer 1s, Ethereum sits at the low end. Chains that set a fixed percentage inflation rate with a scheduled decay mint against total supply directly and typically print several percent a year before fee burns. Ethereum mints against the square root of the staked pool instead, so doubling the stake raises issuance by only about 41%. That square root is the most underrated feature of Ethereum's monetary design, and it is why a staking ratio at an all-time high of roughly **35%** has not produced a matching jump in the mint.

Against fee-burn chains, though, Ethereum is now the weak case. Exchange tokens that burn a share of revenue each quarter produce a burn that scales with the business; Ethereum's burn scales with L1 congestion, and Ethereum has spent three years successfully exporting congestion to rollups. The 2023-era framing of ETH as deflationary money rested on a base fee measured in tens of gwei; at **0.10 gwei** it no longer describes the asset.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, **EIP-8363** (also circulating as EIP-8361), the "Tapered Issuance Burn" draft filed **Aug 4 2026** by six researchers including one from the Ethereum Foundation: it would burn a rising share of validator rewards as the staking ratio climbs, reaching zero net consensus issuance once staked ETH hits about 60.25M. It is a draft that missed its inclusion deadline, so nothing here reflects it — but adoption would be the largest change to ETH issuance since the Merge. Second, **Glamsterdam**, headlined by EIP-7732 enshrined proposer-builder separation and EIP-7928 block-level access lists, whose mainnet target has slipped to **Q4 2026** with no epoch announced; the gas-limit path it opens toward 200M would push the base fee, and the burn, lower still. Third, the **staking ratio**: with the exit queue near empty and roughly 2.48M ETH waiting to enter, the mint rises from here. Fourth, the pace of **Ethereum Foundation tranches** — four 1,000-ETH releases in this window, and any acceleration lands straight in the Foundation sell row. Fifth, any sustained return of L1 congestion, the only thing that can make the burn matter again.

## Summary

Ethereum is mildly and predictably inflationary. Consensus-layer issuance of about **255.7K ETH** per 90 days is the only mint, set by a square-root curve on a staked pool of roughly **42.3M ETH**, and it is offset by an EIP-1559 base-fee burn of only about **3.7K ETH** — around one percent of what is paid out. Net supply moved **+0.211%** over the last 90 days and is projected at **+0.216%** for the next. The main risk sits in the design rather than the calendar: Ethereum's deflationary reputation depends on a burn its own scaling roadmap keeps shrinking, and with no hard cap and no buyback, nothing else stands between issuance and the market. The offsetting fact is that the issuance itself is small, square-root-damped, and — if the tapered-issuance draft ever ships — capable of being cut to zero by protocol rule rather than by promise.
