Ethereum minted about **260.1K ETH** to validators over the last 90 days and destroyed only about **3.9K ETH** through the EIP-1559 base-fee burn — the burn now covers barely one and a half percent of what the protocol pays out. Add roughly **3.2K ETH** drained from Ethereum Foundation wallets and the net is **+0.215%** of supply to market over 90 days, with **+0.219%** projected next. Ethereum has no hard cap and no buyback; the only brake on issuance is the burn, and the burn has been shrinking since rollup data moved to blobspace.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

Against a circulating supply of **120.68M ETH**, the Pressure Framework reads sell pressure of **263.3K ETH** and buy pressure of **3.9K ETH** over the trailing 90 days — a net of **+0.215%**. Projected forward on the live protocol run rate, the next 90 days come to **+0.219%**. The independent supply monitor reads **-0.00086%** for the same window, a gap of **-0.216** percentage points, which sits inside the framework's half-point tolerance, so no data-conflict flag is raised on the ETH page. The monitor derives supply from market capitalisation divided by price and carries roughly a twentieth of a percent of day-to-day rounding noise on an asset this size, which is why its endpoint reading lands near zero while the chain itself plainly added coins; a least-squares fit through the same 90 daily snapshots slopes the same way the chain does. Ethereum is a **mildly inflationary proof-of-stake Layer 1 whose only deflationary mechanism has faded to a rounding error**.

## Sell pressure: where new ETH comes from

Ethereum has exactly one mint, and it is validator issuance on the consensus layer. It is not a fixed block reward: the protocol pays in proportion to the **square root of the total staked pool**, so the more ETH is staked, the more ETH is issued in absolute terms while each staker's yield falls. Reading the full validator set directly off the chain for this analysis returns **903,097 active validators** holding **42.64M ETH** of effective balance — about **35%** of all Ethereum in existence — up from **39.61M ETH** when the window opened. Running the protocol's curve across that rising pool gives **260.1K ETH** of new issuance over the 90 days, roughly **2,890 ETH a day**. Because Ethereum pays its rewards per epoch and epochs advance on a wall clock rather than per block, the 2,436 slots that went empty in this window cost the validator set nothing at all — a detail that quietly breaks the usual assumption that slower blocks mean less issuance.

Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is **zero** and structurally so. Ethereum has no vesting schedule and no unlock contract anywhere: the 2014 crowdsale and both founding endowments were fully distributed by 2017, and no unlock tracker carries an entry for the asset. There is simply no cliff to arrive.

Sell #3 covers the Ethereum Foundation, and here the ledger books **3.2K ETH**. Reading the Foundation's four-address cluster — a main multisig, two disbursing safes and one operational wallet — at both ends of the window shows it falling from **12,562 ETH** to **9,381 ETH**. That drop resolves to exactly three 1,000-ETH tranches leaving the main multisig, on **Jul 15 2026**, **Jul 27 2026** and **Aug 11 2026**, each traced to the safe that received it. Coins that merely moved between Foundation wallets are excluded, because they never left Foundation control. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy supply, is **zero**: the largest crypto estate settles its ether claims in cash rather than in kind, and the in-kind distribution rounds elsewhere have concluded and only ever moved coins that were already circulating.

## Buy pressure: where new ETH goes

Ethereum's only buy-side mechanism is the EIP-1559 base-fee burn, and it accounted for **3.9K ETH** over the window — about **44 ETH a day**. That is a genuine supply reduction rather than a bookkeeping one: the base fee is subtracted from the sender and credited to nobody, so the coins cease to exist at the protocol level. Ethereum uses no burn address for this, which means there is a single surface to measure and no risk of counting the same flow twice. This analysis measured it from execution-layer block headers across the whole window, and cross-checked the result against an independent full-population burn index; the two agree to within one percent over their overlapping month.

The direction matters more than the level. The burn ran at **107 ETH a day** when the window opened and **37 ETH a day** across the most recent month — it now offsets only about **1.5%** of what validators are paid. Base fees sat near a tenth of a gwei against a 60 million gas limit, because rollup data has migrated to cheap blobspace and no longer competes for mainnet gas. The blob-fee burn itself contributes a rounding error. Buy #1, a programmatic buyback, is **zero** and cannot be otherwise: validators are paid in freshly minted ETH rather than purchased ETH, and the protocol holds no treasury it could bid with. Buy #3, a Foundation buy, is **zero** — the Ethereum Foundation is a structural net seller funding research and grants. Buy #4, a new long-term lock, is also **zero**, and that is a deliberate call rather than an oversight: staking keeps growing, but there is no lockup contract with a stated size, no staking cap, stakers can exit at will, and staked ETH remains inside the circulating count. Nothing leaves the float, so the growing stake is counted where it actually acts — on the mint side, pushing issuance up.

## Foundation and overhang

The tracked overhang on Ethereum is small relative to the asset and entirely Foundation-shaped. The Ethereum Foundation cluster still holds **9,381 ETH** in readable wallets, alongside roughly **53K ETH** it has staked, and its published treasury policy fires sales on a periodic shortfall test rather than on a calendar — which is why the framework ships the observed run rate rather than a projected tranche count. Those balances are re-read from the chain on every refresh. If this cluster's balance falls between refreshes, the outflow enters Sell #3 at the next refresh.

One category is deliberately excluded. Listed companies now run very large Ethereum treasury vehicles, and one of them bought ETH directly from the Foundation earlier in 2026. Those positions are not Foundation overhang and not framework sell pressure, because the test is direction rather than actor: the coins were bought on the open market, they already sit inside the circulating denominator, and a corporate treasury buying them removes nothing from supply. By the same logic, a corporate treasury selling them would add nothing to it.

## How ETH compares to other proof-of-stake Layer 1 chains

Ethereum occupies an unusual middle position. Against a hard-capped proof-of-work chain, Ethereum has no terminal supply and no halving — issuance continues indefinitely and, because it scales with the square root of the staked pool, it actually rises in absolute terms as staking adoption grows. That is the opposite of a halving chain's built-in disinflation. Against most uncapped proof-of-stake Layer 1s, though, Ethereum is markedly tighter: a net figure near a fifth of a percent per quarter is far below the mid-single-digit annual issuance common among newer delegated-stake chains, because Ethereum's square-root curve deliberately suppresses the reward rate as participation climbs.

The comparison that really distinguishes Ethereum is the burn. Exchange tokens with quarterly buybacks retire supply out of business revenue, and that mechanism scales with the issuer's profitability. Ethereum's EIP-1559 burn instead scales with congestion on one specific surface — mainnet gas — and Ethereum has spent three years successfully moving activity off that surface onto rollups and blobspace. The burn is therefore structurally counter-cyclical to Ethereum's own scaling roadmap: the better Layer 2 works, the less ETH burns. Chains with a tail emission and no burn at all have a simpler, more predictable profile; Ethereum's is a two-sided mechanism whose deflationary half has been deliberately engineered into irrelevance for normal conditions.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, **EIP-8361**, also circulating as **EIP-8363**, the "Tapered Issuance Burn" filed on **Aug 4 2026**, which would taper validator rewards toward zero as the staking ratio rises. It is a draft, it did not receive proposed-for-inclusion status, and it has no activation epoch — so it books nothing today, but it is the single change that would most alter this page. Second, the **Glamsterdam** upgrade, which the chain's own consensus configuration still reports as unscheduled and which is targeted for late 2026; its headline proposals do not touch issuance, but its gas-repricing cluster could revive mainnet base fees and with them the burn. Third, the staked pool itself: it grew from 39.61M to 42.64M ETH inside this window, and because issuance follows the square root of that pool, continued growth mechanically lifts the sell side. Fourth, the Ethereum Foundation's tranche rhythm, which fired three times in this window with no published calendar. Fifth, whether mainnet gas demand recovers at all — the burn is the only lever that can pull this reading back toward neutral.

## Summary

The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads Ethereum as mildly inflationary and steady: **+0.215%** of supply to market over the trailing 90 days and **+0.219%** projected forward, driven almost entirely by **260.1K ETH** of validator issuance against a base-fee burn of only **3.9K ETH**. The structural mechanism is a square-root issuance curve that pays more in absolute terms as staking grows, paired with a burn that scales with mainnet congestion Ethereum has spent years exporting to rollups. The key risk is that this asymmetry widens rather than closes — staking participation is still climbing while the burn keeps sliding, and no proposal to taper issuance has cleared draft status. Ethereum has no hard cap and no buyback, so there is no ceiling on supply and no mechanism other than the burn to remove it.
