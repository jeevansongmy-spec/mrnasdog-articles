Stellar (XLM) added **+3.05%** to its tradable supply over the last 90 days and is projected to add the same **+3.05%** over the next 90 — even though the Stellar network has not created a single new lumen since **2019**. The whole increase comes from one place: the Stellar Development Foundation released **1,063,555,858.25 XLM** out of its thirteen mandate accounts and into circulation, against a network fee pool that absorbed only **610,515.48 XLM** in the other direction. The independent supply monitor reads **+3.37%** for the same window, a gap of **0.32 percentage points**, so XLM ships with no data-conflict flag. Stellar is not an inflationary chain — it is a fixed pool of lumens with a discretionary distributor, and the distributor is the entire ledger.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

For the 90-day window ending **Sep 7 2026**, the MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads **XLM at +3.05% net**: sell pressure of **1.06B XLM** against buy pressure of **611K XLM**, on a circulating float of **34.80B XLM**. The supply monitor reads the realised 90-day change at **+3.37%**. The gap is **0.32 percentage points**, inside the framework's half-point tolerance, so no conflict flag is raised — the residual is the monitor's own base, which puts the float 90 days ago about **0.28%** lower than the Stellar supply identity does. The forward column is identical, because the mechanism is identical: nothing about the next 90 days is scheduled, so the framework carries the measured pace forward. The label for XLM is **a fixed supply with a discretionary float** — no issuance, no vesting, no burn worth the name, and one foundation deciding how fast the remaining lumens reach the market.

## Sell pressure: where new XLM comes from

Three of the four canonical sell rows are zero, and the fourth is the whole page. Sell #1, protocol inflation, is **zero**: Stellar paid a **1%** a year network inflation for its first five years, and validators voted it off on **Oct 28 2019**. That was tested this window rather than assumed — the issued figure that sits behind Stellar's published supply read the same **105,443,902,087.3472865** lumens at both ends of the 90 days, and it is a figure the retired inflation genuinely used to write to, so a flat reading is a real measurement rather than a number nothing can move. The framework still tags the row as watched rather than closed, because the change that disabled Stellar inflation left the mechanism in place and its own design note says it can be turned back on.

Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is **zero** and always has been. Every lumen that will ever exist was created when Stellar launched, rather than sold into a locked investor cap table, so there is no cliff calendar to run and no unlock tracker carries a Stellar entry. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy supply, is **zero**: Stellar has never had an estate distribution and no long-dated lock is unwinding into the XLM float.

Sell #3 is where all **1,063,555,858.25 XLM** of the window's sell pressure sits. The Stellar Development Foundation holds **14,930,304,662.59 XLM** across thirteen accounts it publishes itself, split into development at **2,004,236,533**, growth at **5,853,159,257**, product and innovation at **3,657,737,252** and assets and liquidity at **3,415,171,620**. Every native movement on all thirteen was walked back across the window, and the aggregate fell by **1,063,555,858.25 XLM** — about **11.8M XLM** a day. That walk was closed against an independent reading of the same aggregate rather than against its own coverage claim, and it lands within **0.01 XLM** on a base of **15.56 billion**. The pace was not smooth: **13.2M** a day to **Jul 9 2026**, then **8.98M** to **Aug 9 2026**, then **13.4M** since. With no trend and no announced change, the forward column holds the 90-day average rather than the hotter recent month.

## Buy pressure: where new XLM goes

The buy side of XLM is almost empty, and that is the mechanical reason the page reads the way it does. Buy #1, programmatic buyback, is **zero**: the Stellar Development Foundation exists to spend lumens it was given at launch, not to buy them back, and no buyback contract or programme exists. Buy #3, foundation buying, is **zero** for the same reason — the net direction of all thirteen Stellar mandate accounts across the window was out, not in. Buy #4, new long-term locks, is **zero** and cannot easily be anything else: Stellar consensus neither pays nor bonds its validators, so about **100** of them across roughly **19** organisations secure the network while holding no XLM for the privilege. There is no staking on Stellar to lock lumens into.

Buy #2, the protocol fee burn, is the only sink, and it is a genuine one. Every Stellar transaction pays at least **100** stroops — a hundred-thousandth of a lumen — and those fees are not paid to validators. They accrue to a network fee pool that belongs to no account and that nobody can spend, so the lumens leave the float. Read from ledger state at both ends of the window, the pool went from **9,931,039.97 XLM** to **10,541,555.46 XLM**, a rise of **610,515.48**, about **6,783 XLM** a day, and an independent read of Stellar network fees lands within **1%** of it. It offsets roughly six ten-thousandths of what the Foundation released. Stellar's famous burn is finished, not ongoing: the keyless account holding the **55.44B** lumens destroyed in **2019** took in **0.21 XLM** across the whole window and sends nothing, and because Stellar's published total supply is defined as the issued figure minus that balance, the two are one reading seen from two sides rather than two mechanisms.

## Foundation and overhang

The Stellar Development Foundation is the only team-controlled XLM overhang, and it is a large one: **14,930,304,662.59 XLM**, about **42.9%** of the circulating float, spread across thirteen named accounts in four buckets. Every one is on-chain and readable, so all thirteen are re-read on each refresh rather than taken on trust, and no published calendar governs any of them — the Foundation says it moves funding between the buckets as the ecosystem needs it. One further balance is tracked without being team-held: a **2015** escrow of **258,885,847.51 XLM** owed one-for-one to holders of the pre-2015 Stellar network, which moved **0.0000025 XLM** across the window and could in principle be claimed into circulation at any time. Exchange custody wallets and unlabelled large holders are excluded by rule — those lumens belong to depositors or to no identified group. If any of these balances falls between refreshes, the outflow enters Sell #3 at the next refresh.

## How XLM compares to other payment-rail chains

XLM sits in an unusual place among payment rails. An uncapped proof-of-stake chain pays its validators in freshly minted coins and hopes a fee burn offsets them; the burn usually loses, so the float grows on autopilot. A capped proof-of-work chain still issues a subsidy until it reaches its ceiling, so its supply also rises, just on a known curve. Stellar does neither. The protocol has been issuing nothing since **2019**, the ceiling is a real **50.00B** lumens, and the float still grows close to **3%** a quarter — because the lumens were all created up front and a foundation is handing them out. A reader used to reading emission curves will read this page wrong: there is no curve to model, only a spending decision.

Against exchange-linked tokens that burn from a reserve, the contrast runs the other way. Those chains shrink their float on a published schedule; Stellar expands its float on an unpublished one. And against the payment rails XLM competes with directly, the fee economy is the honest weak point: Stellar collects roughly **$463,000** a year in network fees against a market capitalisation near **$6.5B**, about **0.0071%**, which places XLM among the quietest fee economies the framework measures — well below the busiest settlement chains, which clear a full percentage point or more. Cheap transactions are the product, so a small fee take is a design choice rather than a failure; the consequence for this page is simply that the sink can never grow large enough to matter against the Foundation's releases.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, the Foundation's release pace: at **11.8M XLM** a day the reading sits barely past a band edge, and a quarter running at the softer **8.98M** a day seen in July would pull the net back under **3%**. Second, the mandate balance itself — **14,930,304,662.59 XLM** is about three and a half years of runway at the current pace, and any published schedule would convert this row from a measurement into a forecast. Third, Stellar protocol upgrades: version **27** activated on **Jul 8 2026** inside this window and did not touch issuance or the **100**-stroop base fee, and the next version carries no mainnet date yet, but any change to Stellar fee handling would move the only sink on the page. Fourth, the retired inflation mechanism: it was disabled rather than deleted, so a validator vote to switch it back on would add a Sell #1 row that has read zero since **2019**. Fifth, the **258,885,847.51 XLM** upgrade escrow, which has been effectively frozen for a decade but is still claimable.

## Summary

The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads XLM at **+3.05% net** over the trailing 90 days and the same **+3.05%** over the next 90. The mechanism is unusual and worth stating plainly: Stellar mints nothing, burns nothing on an ongoing basis and holds a fixed **50.00B** lumens, yet the tradable float still expands close to three percent a quarter because the Stellar Development Foundation released **1.06B XLM** from its thirteen mandate accounts into circulation while the fee pool absorbed only **611K** back. The key risk is that the pace is discretionary — no schedule binds it and no sink offsets it, so the forward number forecasts behaviour rather than code, and it can move in either direction without an announcement. The ceiling is genuine: **14,930,304,662.59 XLM** remains in Foundation custody, and once that is spent the XLM float can only grow again if Stellar validators vote issuance back into the protocol.
