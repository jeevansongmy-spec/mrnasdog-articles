# BTC Inflation Analysis · November 2026 · Mixed flows · supply roughly steady

Bitcoin supply is roughly steady: BTC grew **+0.20%** in the 90 days to Nov 9 2026, and the same pace is expected for the next 90 days. Every new BTC comes from the block subsidy, which paid miners **41,188 BTC** for **13,180 blocks**, while nothing was burned or bought back (**0 BTC**). Bitcoin has a hard cap of **21M BTC**, about **20.11M** already exist, and other trackers show the same **+0.20%**.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

Between Aug 11 and Nov 9 2026, Bitcoin added **41,188 BTC** to a circulating supply of **20,109,909 BTC**, a net change of **+0.20%**. The next 90 days should look the same, **+0.20%**, because the Bitcoin block subsidy is fixed at 3.125 BTC per block until the next halving in 2028. Other trackers read **+0.20%** for the same 90 days, so the two readings agree to within **0.01 points** and the coin page carries no warning note. Bitcoin is a **low, fixed-schedule emission chain**: miners are the only source of new BTC, and no burn, buyback or lock takes any BTC back out.

## Sell pressure: where new BTC comes from

Protocol inflation is the only live sell row. Each Bitcoin block pays the miner who finds it a subsidy of 3.125 BTC, set by the April 2024 halving. From Aug 11 to Nov 9 2026 miners found **13,180 blocks**, so the subsidy created **41,188 BTC**, about **458 BTC a day**. Blocks came a little faster than the 10-minute target, about one every 9 minutes 50 seconds, because more mining power joined faster than the difficulty adjustment could catch up. That is why the 90-day total sits slightly above the 40,500 BTC a perfect 10-minute pace would give.

Vesting unlocks are **0 BTC**. Bitcoin launched in January 2009 with no presale, no team allocation and no investor allocation, so there is no vesting calendar and no locked batch of BTC that can open.

Foundation and unscheduled unlocks are **0 BTC**. Bitcoin has no foundation, no company and no treasury holding a reserve. The roughly 1.1M BTC mined in Bitcoin’s first year and never moved still sits in its original wallets, but those coins already count as circulating, so moving them would not add to the supply count.

Long-term locked or bankruptcy supply is **0 BTC**, even though the biggest bankruptcy in Bitcoin history is still open. The Mt. Gox estate holds about **34,388 BTC**, and on Oct 16 2026 its trustee moved the repayment deadline from Oct 31 2026 to **Oct 31 2027**. The US government moved about **17,795** seized BTC between Oct 6 and Oct 8 2026, and the UK still holds about 61,000 seized BTC. Every one of these coins was mined years ago and already counts as circulating, so a payout or a sale moves BTC from one holder to another without creating any.

## Buy pressure: where new BTC goes

Programmatic buyback is **0 BTC**. Bitcoin has no issuer, so there is no contract or treasury that buys BTC back for the network.

The protocol fee burn is **0 BTC**. Bitcoin transaction fees are paid to miners on top of the block subsidy, and no Bitcoin rule destroys any part of them. People still send small amounts to well-known dead addresses by choice: about **0.0038 BTC** went to the two best-known ones between Aug 11 and Nov 9 2026, far too little to change the supply count.

Foundation buying is **0 BTC**. The large Bitcoin buyers are companies and funds, not a foundation. Strategy bought another 1,036 BTC on Nov 9 2026 and now holds about **850,364 BTC**, and US spot Bitcoin funds saw about $1.28 billion of withdrawals from Nov 2 to Nov 6 2026. Both trade BTC that is already circulating, so their buying and selling moves price, not supply.

New long-term locks are **0 BTC**. Bitcoin has no staking, so no BTC is locked to earn rewards. A draft proposal to one day freeze old Bitcoin coins that a future quantum computer could steal has no start date and is not part of any software release.

## Foundation and overhang

Bitcoin has no team-controlled overhang: no foundation wallet, no team multisig, no unscheduled unlock pool and no buyback wallet. The large known holders are third parties whose coins already count as circulating. The Mt. Gox estate holds about **34,388 BTC** with a deadline of Oct 31 2027. The US government moved about **17,795 BTC** of seized coins in early October 2026 without a confirmed sale. The UK holds about **61,000 BTC** seized in a fraud case. The thief behind the Oct 29 2026 Binance hack took about **18,270 BTC**. We re-check these holders about every two weeks. When any of them sells, the price can feel it, but the Bitcoin supply count stays the same, because these coins already count. If a holder that sits outside the circulating count ever appears and its balance falls between our checks, the outflow enters the foundation and unscheduled unlocks row at the next check.

## How BTC compares to other proof-of-work chains with a hard cap

Bitcoin is the original halving-model chain: a fixed block subsidy, a hard cap of 21M BTC and a halving every 210,000 blocks, about every four years. At about **+0.20%** per 90 days, or roughly 0.8% a year, Bitcoin now prints less new supply each year than gold mining adds to the world’s gold stock. Litecoin uses the same design with an 84M cap and its own halvings, so its rate falls in the same steps. Bitcoin Cash copied Bitcoin’s schedule exactly, so its issuance tracks Bitcoin’s block for block.

The contrast is sharper with chains that have no cap. Dogecoin pays a fixed 10,000 DOGE per block forever, so its new supply never halves. Monero ended its main emission in 2022 and now pays a small fixed tail emission of 0.6 XMR per block to keep miners paid. Ethereum moved to proof of stake and pays validators in new ETH while burning part of every fee, so its supply rises or falls with network use. Bitcoin has neither a tail emission nor a fee burn: its new supply depends only on the block count, and it heads toward zero as the halvings continue.

Bitcoin also has no investor unlocks, which sets it apart from most newer coins whose supply jumps when team and investor allocations open. For Bitcoin, the big supply questions are about who holds existing coins, like the Mt. Gox estate and government wallets, not about new coins entering the count.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

**Nov 10 2026:** Binance closes its Bitcoin mining pool. The miners on it will move to other pools; this changes who finds blocks, not how many BTC each block pays.

**Around Nov 13 2026:** the next Bitcoin difficulty adjustment at block 975,744. If blocks keep coming faster than every 10 minutes, the difficulty rises and pulls the pace back toward the target, which would trim the 90-day subsidy slightly.

**Mt. Gox wallets, through Oct 31 2027:** the estate’s roughly 34,388 BTC can move to exchanges for creditor payouts at any time before the deadline. A payout would not change Bitcoin’s supply, but the transfers are worth watching as possible selling.

**Government and hacker wallets:** the US government’s seized coins moved in October 2026, and the Binance hacker still controls about 18,270 BTC. Sales would add selling, not supply.

**Quantum debate:** the draft plan to freeze Bitcoin coins that a quantum computer could steal is still a draft. If it ever gets a start date, it would be the first Bitcoin rule change to take coins out of reach, and we would book it the day it is scheduled.

## Summary

Bitcoin’s supply grew **+0.20%** in the 90 days to Nov 9 2026, from **41,188 BTC** paid to miners and nothing burned, bought back or locked. The Bitcoin block subsidy is fixed at 3.125 BTC per block until the next halving at block 1,050,000, around April 2028, so the next 90 days should look the same. The main supply-side risk is selling of coins that already exist, such as the Mt. Gox estate’s roughly 34,388 BTC, not new coins. With about 20.11M BTC out of a hard cap of 21M BTC, Bitcoin has only about 890,000 BTC left to mine, ever.
