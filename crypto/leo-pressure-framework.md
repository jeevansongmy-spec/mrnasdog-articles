# LEO Inflation Analysis · September 2026 · Mixed flows, supply roughly steady

UNUS SED LEO, the exchange token of Bitfinex and its parent company iFinex, mints nothing and never had a vesting calendar: no new LEO appeared on Ethereum or on Vaulta in the last 90 days. Its one supply flow is a revenue-funded LEO buyback that moved **442,970 LEO** into the token's own issuer account, where it sits outside the circulating count — parked, not destroyed. That is **0.44M LEO** removed against **zero** added, so the MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads LEO at **−0.05% net** over the last 90 days against a supply-monitor reading of **−0.12%** — a gap of **0.07 percentage points**, which is agreement, not conflict. LEO supply is roughly steady and edging down, with both mint paths still switched on.

## The verdict, in one paragraph

For the 90-day window ending **Sep 25 2026**, the Pressure Framework reads **LEO at −0.05% net**: nothing on the sell side adds a single LEO, while the LEO buyback took **442,970 LEO** out of the circulating float of **919.86M LEO**. The independent supply monitor reads the realised 90-day change at **−0.12%**. The gap is **0.07 percentage points**, far inside the framework's half-point tolerance, so LEO ships with **no data-conflict flag**. The forward column also reads **−0.05%**, because the buyback runs every day and the framework carries its trailing pace forward. The label for LEO is **slowly shrinking by reversible buyback**: a corporate token with zero issuance whose float falls a little each day, not through destruction but through coins set aside in an account the issuer still controls.

## Sell pressure: where new LEO comes from

In this window, nowhere. Sell #1, protocol inflation, is **zero**. LEO lives on two chains — **660M LEO** as an ERC-20 token on Ethereum and **307.65M LEO** on Vaulta, the chain formerly called EOS — and neither leg grew. The Ethereum supply read exactly **660M** at both ends of the window, and that figure is held in the contract's own storage rather than compiled in, so the flat reading is a real measurement. The Vaulta supply did not change either, and neither history index of the contract shows an issue action in the window. What keeps this a checked zero rather than a permanent one is that the mint switch still exists on both chains: an Ethereum controller contract can create LEO, and a two-of-three Bitfinex multisig can issue up to **692.35M LEO** more on Vaulta. The framework tested that directly rather than trusting documents — a simulated mint call from the controller succeeds, while the same call from anyone else fails.

Sell #2, vesting unlocks, is **zero** because there was never a vesting calendar: all one billion LEO were sold in a single private sale in **May 2019**, and no unlock tracker carries a LEO schedule. Sell #3, foundation and unscheduled unlocks, is **zero**, and it was measured. The largest team balances — a Bitfinex multisig holding **648M LEO** on Ethereum and a Vaulta cold account holding **257.79M LEO** — did not move in 90 days, and both already count as circulating LEO, so even a sale from either would move coins inside the float rather than add new LEO supply. Sell #4, long-term locked or bankruptcy supply, is **zero**: no estate or trustee holds LEO. The bitcoin seized after the 2016 Bitfinex hack is a bitcoin claim, not a LEO one.

## Buy pressure: where new LEO goes

Buy #1, the programmatic LEO buyback, carries the whole ledger. iFinex committed in its 2019 whitepaper to spend at least **27%** of its monthly consolidated gross revenue buying LEO back. On chain, the bought LEO leaves a Bitfinex funding account once a day and lands in the token contract's own account on Vaulta. Over the window that came to **442,970 LEO**, and the figure closes exactly five independent ways: the issuer account's balance rose by that amount, two separate Vaulta history indexers each list the same 62 transfers summing to it, the funding account fell by the same amount, and iFinex's own published LEO supply fell by it too. Bitfinex labels these transfers a burn, but they are not one in the strict sense — no LEO was retired and neither chain's supply fell. The LEO is parked in an account the issuer controls. It still counts as removed, because the circulating LEO figure is defined as total supply minus exactly that account's balance, so every coin that lands there leaves the float once.

The LEO buyback is steady rather than smooth. Daily transfers ran at roughly **6,300 to 7,400 LEO**, stopped from **Jun 29 2026** to **Jul 4 2026**, and have been paused again since **Sep 1 2026** while buying continued: about **116K LEO** is bought and waiting to move. Earlier pauses were cleared in one transfer — **384,919 LEO** on **Sep 15 2025** and **228,754 LEO** on **Feb 4 2026** — each matching its backlog, so the framework treats them as routine catch-ups, not as a separate event. The forward column keeps the trailing **442,970 LEO** and does not add the waiting backlog. Buy #2, a protocol fee burn, is **zero**: neither Ethereum nor Vaulta burns LEO as part of its transaction fees. Buy #3, foundation buying, is **zero** beyond the buyback already counted. Buy #4, new long-term locks, is **zero**: no new LEO lockup with a stated size appeared this window. Buy #5, the recovered-funds LEO buyback, is **zero** for now: iFinex has promised at least **80%** of any net bitcoin recovered from the 2016 hack to extra LEO buybacks over about eighteen months, but the seized wallet still holds **94,643 BTC** with nothing spent.

## Foundation and overhang

Four iFinex-controlled LEO balances are tracked, each read on chain at every refresh. The parked account holds **47.80M LEO**, the only LEO balance outside the circulating count; if any of it ever flows back out, it becomes new supply. The Bitfinex Ethereum multisig holds **648M LEO** and did not move in the window. The Vaulta cold account holds **257.79M LEO** and has not moved since September 2025. The funding account that feeds the LEO buyback holds **2.06M LEO**, down from **2.51M** at the start of the window. The two large balances sit inside the float, so the framework watches them without booking them. Exchange hot wallets holding customer deposits are left out by rule. There is also unissued headroom: the Vaulta contract can still issue up to **692.35M LEO** under its one-billion supply cap. If the parked balance falls between refreshes, or any new LEO is issued, the flow enters Sell #3 or Sell #1 at the next refresh.

## How LEO compares to other exchange tokens

LEO belongs to the group of exchange tokens whose supply is managed by the company behind the exchange rather than by a chain's rules. The clearest contrast is with an exchange token that is also a chain's gas coin and destroys supply for real: there, a quarterly event or a gas-fee burn sends coins to an address nobody controls, and published supply falls for good. LEO's buyback removes coins from the float every day, but it parks them in an account the issuer can still move, so the reduction is real for the float and reversible in principle. LEO also kept its mint switches, so its supply is neither fixed nor guaranteed to fall.

Against general-purpose chains that pay validators with fresh coins, LEO has the opposite shape: no issuance at all, and a small, steady float reduction funded by company revenue. The trade-off is scale and dependence. At **0.05%** a quarter the LEO buyback moves the float slowly, and its size follows iFinex revenue rather than any protocol rule, so a weaker quarter for the exchange means a smaller buyback. The one thing that could change that scale is the recovered 2016 bitcoin, which would fund a far larger LEO buyback if it is ever released.

## What to watch in the next 90 days

First, the paused transfers: LEO moves into the parked account stopped on **Sep 1 2026** with about **116K LEO** waiting, and a catch-up transfer would confirm the pattern of earlier pauses. Second, the 2016 hack bitcoin: the **94,643 BTC** still sits unspent in the seized wallet, and any transfer to Bitfinex would start the promised recovered-funds LEO buyback and could change the forward reading by far more than the routine buyback does. Third, the parked account at **47.80M LEO**: an outflow would open a sell row that has been zero all window. Fourth, the funding account at **2.06M LEO**, which at the current pace covers a little over a year of transfers — how iFinex refills it will show whether bought LEO keeps flowing through the same path.

## Summary

The MrNasdog Pressure Framework reads LEO at **−0.05% net** over the trailing 90 days and **−0.05%** over the next 90, on a sell side that is zero across all four canonical rows. The mechanism is a corporate token that mints nothing, paired with a once-a-day, revenue-funded LEO buyback that parked **442,970 LEO** in the issuer's own account in the window. The key risk is that the buyback is reversible and the mint switches are live on both chains, so the reading rests on iFinex's continued choices rather than on code that cannot change. The upside case is the unreleased 2016 hack bitcoin, which is promised to a much larger LEO buyback if it ever reaches Bitfinex.
